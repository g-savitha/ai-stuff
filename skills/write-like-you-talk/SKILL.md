# Write Like You Talk

## Choose the audience

Determine whether the user is speaking to:

1. A peer at work
2. A LinkedIn audience
3. A friend who is curious about technology

When the audience is unspecified and would materially change the writing, ask who the post is for. Otherwise, default to a senior engineer informally mentoring another engineer.

## Write a plain draft first (ASD-STE100)

Before adding any voice, write or rewrite the content in ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English. This pass makes the ideas clear and correct. The next pass makes them sound human.

- Keep sentences short: at most 20 words for instructions and 25 for descriptions, and usually fewer.
- Put one idea in each sentence and one topic in each paragraph.
- Use the active voice and name who or what does the action.
- Use simple, common words, and use each word with one meaning only.
- Use the same term for the same thing every time. Do not switch between synonyms.
- Write instructions as commands, in the order the reader does them.
- Keep the needed technical names, and explain each one the first time it appears.
- Remove filler, hedging stacks, and words that add no meaning.

Check the facts in this draft against the sources before you continue. Then rewrite it with the conversational voice below. Keep the STE clarity underneath: the final text can be relaxed, but it must stay short, plain, and exact.

## Use a conversational voice

Make the writing feel like a thoughtful conversation with someone knowledgeable but approachable.

- Use language people naturally use while speaking.
- Keep paragraphs short, usually one to three sentences.
- Prefer familiar words such as `use`, `help`, `improve`, `understand`, `build`, `try`, and `problem`.
- Use technical terms when they add useful precision, and explain unfamiliar terms naturally.
- Share observations and reasoning instead of presenting every opinion as unquestionable fact.
- Use rhetorical questions sparingly.

Prefer:

> I initially thought these two approaches were solving the same problem. But once I started comparing them, the difference became clearer.

Avoid:

> Upon conducting a comprehensive comparison, it became evident that the two approaches address fundamentally different concerns.

## Preserve the user's point of view

Retain the user's opinions, doubts, reasoning, confidence, and natural expressions when they work well. Clean up the writing without replacing their personality with generic content.

Never invent personal experiences, workplace incidents, conversations, results, metrics, feelings, or lessons. When details are missing, explain the idea directly.

Do not add jokes, memes, sarcasm, or playful lines unless requested. Natural warmth is enough.

## Avoid generic phrasing

Remove corporate and AI-generated expressions such as:

- In today's rapidly evolving landscape
- It is important to note
- Let us delve into
- This groundbreaking technology
- A game changer
- Unlock the power of
- In conclusion
- The future is here
- This is not just X, it is Y
- Whether you are a beginner or an expert

Prefer direct openings and concrete observations. Minimize em dashes by using full stops, commas, parentheses, or separate sentences.

## Shape the post naturally

Do not force every post into the same template. A useful flow is:

1. Start with an observation, confusion, or question.
2. Explain what prompted the thought.
3. Break down the idea simply.
4. Connect it to a practical situation.
5. End with the main takeaway or an honest open question.

Create curiosity without clickbait. End naturally instead of using a formal conclusion.

### For LinkedIn posts

- Start with a clear observation or surprising thought.
- Keep paragraphs short.
- Avoid excessive headings and emojis.
- Avoid engagement bait.
- Add a closing question only when it genuinely supports the post.
- Use few relevant hashtags or omit them unless requested.

### For blog posts

- Explain ideas with enough depth.
- Use natural, useful headings.
- Add examples when they improve understanding.
- Maintain a conversational flow between sections.
- Avoid repeating the same idea in different words.
- Preserve technical accuracy while making the language casual.

## Review before returning

Check that:

- The plain STE draft was written first and its clarity survived the rewrite.
- The result sounds like something a real person would say.
- The voice resembles an experienced engineer speaking to another person.
- No sentence is unnecessarily formal or corporate.
- Paragraphs are easy to scan.
- No experiences, facts, or emotions were invented.
- No unrequested humor was added.
- Em dashes are rare.
- Technical meaning remains accurate.
- The user's actual point of view is still present.
